# Poisson Brackets

## Overview

Poisson brackets are algebraic operations on functions in phase space.

They are useful because they package Hamiltonian mechanics into one operation. Instead of separately remembering Hamilton's equations, conservation tests, symmetry generators, and canonical-coordinate checks, the Poisson bracket gives one language for all of them.

The short version:

- The Hamiltonian $H$ generates time evolution.
- A function $G(q,p)$ can generate a transformation.
- The Poisson bracket tells how one phase-space quantity changes under another quantity's generated motion.

So $\lbrace f,H\rbrace$ means: how does $f$ change as the system evolves in time under $H$?

And $\lbrace f,G\rbrace$ means: how does $f$ change under the transformation generated by $G$?

## Motivation

Hamilton's equations say:

$$
\dot{q}_i=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}
$$

and:

$$
\dot{p}_i=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}
$$

These equations tell us how the basic coordinates $q_i$ and momenta $p_i$ move. But often we care about some other phase-space function:

$$
f(q,p,t)
$$

For example, $f$ might be energy, angular momentum, a component of momentum, or some more complicated quantity. The natural question is:

How does $f$ change along the motion?

Use the chain rule:

$$
\frac{df}{dt}=\sum_i \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}\dot{q}_i+\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\dot{p}_i\right)+\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}
$$

Now substitute Hamilton's equations:

$$
\frac{df}{dt}=\sum_i \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}-\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}\right)+\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}
$$

The long sum appears so often that we give it a name:

$$
\lbrace f,H\rbrace=\sum_i \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}-\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}\right)
$$

So time evolution becomes:

$$
\frac{df}{dt}=\lbrace f,H\rbrace+\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}
$$

This is the first big motivation for Poisson brackets: they are the chain rule plus Hamilton's equations, compressed into an algebraic operation.

## What The Bracket Measures

The bracket $\lbrace f,g\rbrace$ measures how $f$ changes under the flow generated by $g$.

Important special cases:

- $\lbrace f,H\rbrace$ measures how $f$ changes in time.
- $\lbrace f,p_i\rbrace$ measures how $f$ changes under translations in $q_i$.
- $\lbrace f,L_i\rbrace$ measures how $f$ changes under rotations about the $i$ axis.

If:

$$
\lbrace f,H\rbrace=0
$$

and $f$ has no explicit time dependence, then $f$ is conserved.

If:

$$
\lbrace H,G\rbrace=0
$$

then the transformation generated by $G$ is a symmetry of the Hamiltonian.

## Definition

For functions $f(q,p)$ and $g(q,p)$:

$$
\lbrace f,g\rbrace = \sum_i \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial p_i} - \frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial q_i}\right)
$$

The order matters. With this convention, the function in the second slot generates the motion of the function in the first slot:

$$
\delta f=\epsilon\lbrace f,g\rbrace
$$

Reversing the order reverses the sign because $\lbrace g,f\rbrace=-\lbrace f,g\rbrace$. This is the source of many otherwise mysterious minus signs.

### One-Degree-Of-Freedom Picture

For one canonical pair $(q,p)$, the definition reduces to:

$$
\lbrace f,g\rbrace=\frac{\partial f}{\partial q}\frac{\partial g}{\partial p}-\frac{\partial f}{\partial p}\frac{\partial g}{\partial q}
$$

The derivatives of $g$ determine a vector field in phase space:

$$
X_g=\left(\frac{\partial g}{\partial p},-\frac{\partial g}{\partial q}\right)
$$

The bracket is the directional derivative of $f$ along that field:

$$
\lbrace f,g\rbrace=\frac{\partial f}{\partial q}(X_g)_q+\frac{\partial f}{\partial p}(X_g)_p
$$

Thus $g$ does more than assign a number to each phase-space point: it defines a possible flow through phase space. When $g=H$, this is the physical time-evolution flow. When $g$ is another generator, it is a different canonical flow, such as a translation or rotation.

### Symplectic Form

Collect the phase-space coordinates into:

$$
z=(q_1,\ldots,q_n,p_1,\ldots,p_n)^T
$$

and define:

$$
J=\begin{bmatrix}0&I\\-I&0\end{bmatrix}
$$

Then the bracket can be written compactly as:

$$
\lbrace f,g\rbrace=(\nabla f)^T J\nabla g
$$

The matrix $J$ pairs each coordinate with its conjugate momentum and supplies the sign difference in Hamilton's equations. This form also makes clear why canonical transformations are the transformations that preserve Poisson brackets: they preserve this coordinate-momentum pairing.

## Key Equations

Time evolution can be written:

$$
\frac{df}{dt} = \lbrace f,H\rbrace + \frac{\partial f}{\partial t}
$$

The fundamental brackets are:

$$
\lbrace q_i,p_j\rbrace = \delta_{ij}
$$

$$
\lbrace q_i,q_j\rbrace = 0
$$

$$
\lbrace p_i,p_j\rbrace = 0
$$

## How To Use Poisson Brackets

### A Reliable Calculation Pattern

For a direct calculation, especially with several degrees of freedom:

1. List the canonical pairs $(q_i,p_i)$.
2. Differentiate both functions with respect to every $q_i$ and $p_i$.
3. For each pair, form $(\partial f/\partial q_i)(\partial g/\partial p_i)-(\partial f/\partial p_i)(\partial g/\partial q_i)$.
4. Sum over $i$.
5. Check the sign by testing a fundamental bracket such as $\lbrace q_i,p_i\rbrace=1$.

Only matching canonical pairs contribute. For example, $\lbrace q_1,p_2\rbrace=0$ because $q_1$ and $p_2$ belong to different pairs.

### To Check Whether A Quantity Is Conserved

1. Identify the Hamiltonian $H$.
2. Identify the candidate conserved quantity $f$.
3. Compute $\lbrace f,H\rbrace$.
4. Include explicit time dependence if present:

$$
\frac{df}{dt}=\lbrace f,H\rbrace+\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}
$$

If this is zero, then $f$ is conserved.

Example: if $H$ does not depend on $x$, then:

$$
\dot{p}_x=\lbrace p_x,H\rbrace=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial x}=0
$$

So $p_x$ is conserved.

### To Find What A Generator Does

If $G$ generates a small transformation with parameter $\epsilon$, then:

$$
\delta f=\epsilon \lbrace f,G\rbrace
$$

Example: momentum generates position translations:

$$
\delta x=\epsilon \lbrace x,p\rbrace=\epsilon
$$

and:

$$
\delta p=\epsilon \lbrace p,p\rbrace=0
$$

So the generator $p$ shifts $x$ while leaving $p$ unchanged.

### To Connect Symmetries To Conservation Laws

If $G$ generates a transformation and the Hamiltonian is invariant under that transformation, then:

$$
\delta H=\epsilon \lbrace H,G\rbrace=0
$$

Therefore:

$$
\lbrace G,H\rbrace=0
$$

If $G$ has no explicit time dependence, then:

$$
\frac{dG}{dt}=0
$$

So $G$ is conserved.

### To Test Canonical Coordinates

A change of variables from $(q,p)$ to $(Q,P)$ is canonical when the new variables preserve the fundamental Poisson bracket structure:

$$
\lbrace Q_i,P_j\rbrace=\delta_{ij}
$$

and:

$$
\lbrace Q_i,Q_j\rbrace=0
$$

and:

$$
\lbrace P_i,P_j\rbrace=0
$$

This is why Poisson brackets are central to canonical transformations: they tell whether the new coordinates still behave like legitimate Hamiltonian coordinates.

## Worked Examples

### Recovering The Harmonic-Oscillator Equations

For one degree of freedom:

$$
H(x,p)=\frac{p^2}{2m}+\frac{1}{2}m\omega^2x^2
$$

The position bracket is:

$$
\lbrace x,H\rbrace=\frac{\partial x}{\partial x}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}-\frac{\partial x}{\partial p}\frac{\partial H}{\partial x}=\frac{p}{m}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\dot{x}=\frac{p}{m}
$$

For momentum:

$$
\lbrace p,H\rbrace=\frac{\partial p}{\partial x}\frac{\partial H}{\partial p}-\frac{\partial p}{\partial p}\frac{\partial H}{\partial x}=-m\omega^2x
$$

Therefore:

$$
\dot{p}=-m\omega^2x
$$

The single equation $\dot f=\lbrace f,H\rbrace$ contains both Hamilton equations; choosing $f=x$ or $f=p$ extracts the desired component.

### Checking Energy Conservation

For an autonomous Hamiltonian, set $f=H$:

$$
\frac{dH}{dt}=\lbrace H,H\rbrace+\frac{\partial H}{\partial t}
$$

Antisymmetry implies $\lbrace H,H\rbrace=0$. If $\partial H/\partial t=0$, then:

$$
\frac{dH}{dt}=0
$$

This explains precisely why an explicitly time-independent Hamiltonian is conserved. It does not rely on simply assuming that every Hamiltonian is automatically constant.

### Momentum As A Translation Generator

In one dimension, let $G=p$. For an arbitrary phase-space function $f(x,p)$:

$$
\lbrace f,p\rbrace=\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}
$$

Therefore an infinitesimal transformation generated by $p$ gives:

$$
\delta f=\epsilon\frac{\partial f}{\partial x}
$$

This is the first-order change produced by shifting $x$ to $x+\epsilon$. In particular:

$$
\delta x=\epsilon,\quad \delta p=0
$$

This is what it means to say that momentum generates spatial translations.

### Angular Momentum As A Rotation Generator

For Cartesian coordinates, define:

$$
L_z=xp_y-yp_x
$$

Then:

$$
\lbrace x,L_z\rbrace=-y,\quad \lbrace y,L_z\rbrace=x
$$

so the transformation generated by $L_z$ is:

$$
\delta x=-\epsilon y,\quad \delta y=\epsilon x
$$

These are the infinitesimal formulas for a counterclockwise rotation in the $xy$ plane. The corresponding momentum components rotate in the same way:

$$
\delta p_x=-\epsilon p_y,\quad \delta p_y=\epsilon p_x
$$

If $\lbrace H,L_z\rbrace=0$, the Hamiltonian is rotationally invariant about the $z$ axis and $L_z$ is conserved.

## Useful Identities

For phase-space functions $f,g,h$ and constants $a,b$:

| Identity | Formula |
| --- | --- |
| Bilinearity | $\lbrace af+bg,h\rbrace=a\lbrace f,h\rbrace+b\lbrace g,h\rbrace$ |
| Antisymmetry | $\lbrace f,g\rbrace=-\lbrace g,f\rbrace$ |
| Product rule | $\lbrace fg,h\rbrace=f\lbrace g,h\rbrace+g\lbrace f,h\rbrace$ |
| Jacobi identity | $\lbrace f,\lbrace g,h\rbrace\rbrace+\lbrace g,\lbrace h,f\rbrace\rbrace+\lbrace h,\lbrace f,g\rbrace\rbrace=0$ |
| Coordinate derivative | $\lbrace q_i,f\rbrace=\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}$ |
| Momentum derivative | $\lbrace p_i,f\rbrace=-\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}$ |

## Proofs Of The Basic Identities

Start from the definition:

$$
\lbrace f,g\rbrace = \sum_i \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial p_i} - \frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial q_i}\right)
$$

### Bilinearity

Compute the bracket of $af+bg$ with $h$:

$$
\lbrace af+bg,h\rbrace=\sum_i \left(\frac{\partial(af+bg)}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial h}{\partial p_i}-\frac{\partial(af+bg)}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial h}{\partial q_i}\right)
$$

Use linearity of partial derivatives:

$$
\frac{\partial(af+bg)}{\partial q_i}=a\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}+b\frac{\partial g}{\partial q_i}
$$

and:

$$
\frac{\partial(af+bg)}{\partial p_i}=a\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}+b\frac{\partial g}{\partial p_i}
$$

Substituting and grouping terms gives:

$$
\lbrace af+bg,h\rbrace=a\lbrace f,h\rbrace+b\lbrace g,h\rbrace
$$

The same argument works in the second slot, so the Poisson bracket is linear in each argument.

### Antisymmetry

Swap $f$ and $g$ in the definition:

$$
\lbrace g,f\rbrace=\sum_i \left(\frac{\partial g}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}-\frac{\partial g}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}\right)
$$

Since ordinary multiplication of functions commutes, rewrite the two products:

$$
\lbrace g,f\rbrace=\sum_i \left(\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial q_i}-\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial g}{\partial p_i}\right)
$$

This is exactly the negative of $\lbrace f,g\rbrace$:

$$
\lbrace g,f\rbrace=-\lbrace f,g\rbrace
$$

So:

$$
\lbrace f,g\rbrace=-\lbrace g,f\rbrace
$$

### Product Rule

Compute $\lbrace fg,h\rbrace$:

$$
\lbrace fg,h\rbrace=\sum_i \left(\frac{\partial(fg)}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial h}{\partial p_i}-\frac{\partial(fg)}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial h}{\partial q_i}\right)
$$

Use the ordinary product rule:

$$
\frac{\partial(fg)}{\partial q_i}=f\frac{\partial g}{\partial q_i}+g\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}
$$

and:

$$
\frac{\partial(fg)}{\partial p_i}=f\frac{\partial g}{\partial p_i}+g\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}
$$

Substitute:

$$
\lbrace fg,h\rbrace=\sum_i \left(f\frac{\partial g}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial h}{\partial p_i}+g\frac{\partial f}{\partial q_i}\frac{\partial h}{\partial p_i}-f\frac{\partial g}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial h}{\partial q_i}-g\frac{\partial f}{\partial p_i}\frac{\partial h}{\partial q_i}\right)
$$

Group the terms multiplied by $f$ and $g$:

$$
\lbrace fg,h\rbrace=f\lbrace g,h\rbrace+g\lbrace f,h\rbrace
$$

Using antisymmetry, the product rule in the second slot is:

$$
\lbrace f,gh\rbrace=g\lbrace f,h\rbrace+h\lbrace f,g\rbrace
$$

### Fundamental Brackets

For the coordinates themselves:

$$
\lbrace q_i,q_j\rbrace=\sum_k \left(\frac{\partial q_i}{\partial q_k}\frac{\partial q_j}{\partial p_k}-\frac{\partial q_i}{\partial p_k}\frac{\partial q_j}{\partial q_k}\right)
$$

Use:

$$
\frac{\partial q_i}{\partial q_k}=\delta_{ik}
$$

and:

$$
\frac{\partial q_i}{\partial p_k}=0
$$

Since $q_j$ has no $p_k$ dependence:

$$
\lbrace q_i,q_j\rbrace=0
$$

For two momenta, the same reasoning gives:

$$
\lbrace p_i,p_j\rbrace=0
$$

For position and momentum:

$$
\lbrace q_i,p_j\rbrace=\sum_k \left(\frac{\partial q_i}{\partial q_k}\frac{\partial p_j}{\partial p_k}-\frac{\partial q_i}{\partial p_k}\frac{\partial p_j}{\partial q_k}\right)
$$

Use:

$$
\frac{\partial q_i}{\partial q_k}=\delta_{ik}
$$

and:

$$
\frac{\partial p_j}{\partial p_k}=\delta_{jk}
$$

The second term vanishes, so:

$$
\lbrace q_i,p_j\rbrace=\sum_k \delta_{ik}\delta_{jk}=\delta_{ij}
$$

### Brackets With Coordinates And Momenta

Set $f=q_i$ in the definition:

$$
\lbrace q_i,g\rbrace=\sum_k \left(\frac{\partial q_i}{\partial q_k}\frac{\partial g}{\partial p_k}-\frac{\partial q_i}{\partial p_k}\frac{\partial g}{\partial q_k}\right)
$$

Using $\partial q_i/\partial q_k=\delta_{ik}$ and $\partial q_i/\partial p_k=0$ gives:

$$
\lbrace q_i,g\rbrace=\frac{\partial g}{\partial p_i}
$$

Similarly:

$$
\lbrace p_i,g\rbrace=-\frac{\partial g}{\partial q_i}
$$

These identities are a compact way to recover Hamilton's equations:

$$
\dot{q}_i=\lbrace q_i,H\rbrace=\frac{\partial H}{\partial p_i}
$$

and:

$$
\dot{p}_i=\lbrace p_i,H\rbrace=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial q_i}
$$

### Jacobi Identity

The Jacobi identity is:

$$
\lbrace f,\lbrace g,h\rbrace\rbrace+\lbrace g,\lbrace h,f\rbrace\rbrace+\lbrace h,\lbrace f,g\rbrace\rbrace=0
$$

A direct proof comes from substituting the definition of the Poisson bracket into each term and expanding. Every term contains one second derivative and two first derivatives. The terms cancel in pairs because mixed partial derivatives commute:

$$
\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial q_i \partial p_j}=\frac{\partial^2 f}{\partial p_j \partial q_i}
$$

Conceptually, the Jacobi identity says that Poisson brackets have a consistent algebraic structure. This is why the Poisson bracket can serve as the classical analogue of the commutator algebra in quantum mechanics.

It also controls how generated transformations combine. Define the action of a generator $g$ on any function by $D_gf=\lbrace f,g\rbrace$. The difference between applying the transformations generated by $g$ and $h$ in opposite orders is itself a generated transformation:

$$
(D_gD_h-D_hD_g)f=D_{\lbrace h,g\rbrace}f
$$

The Jacobi identity guarantees this closure. Translations and rotations can therefore be studied as an algebra of generators rather than as an unrelated list of coordinate formulas.

## Identities For Time Evolution

For any function $f(q,p,t)$:

$$
\frac{df}{dt}=\lbrace f,H\rbrace+\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}
$$

If $f$ has no explicit time dependence, then:

$$
\frac{df}{dt}=\lbrace f,H\rbrace
$$

So $f$ is conserved when:

$$
\lbrace f,H\rbrace=0
$$

This is the identity behind the symmetry-conservation connection in Hamiltonian mechanics.

More generally, explicit time dependence matters. A function can be conserved even when $\lbrace f,H\rbrace$ is not zero, provided:

$$
\lbrace f,H\rbrace+\frac{\partial f}{\partial t}=0
$$

The statement "$f$ is conserved exactly when $\lbrace f,H\rbrace=0$" is valid only when $f$ has no explicit time dependence.

## Common Confusions

- The bracket is evaluated at a phase-space point; it is itself another phase-space function, not automatically a constant.
- The order of the arguments matters. Time evolution is $\dot f=\lbrace f,H\rbrace+\partial f/\partial t$, not $\lbrace H,f\rbrace$ with this convention.
- A zero bracket does not mean two functions are individually constant. It means their generated flows are compatible in the relevant sense; $\lbrace f,H\rbrace=0$ gives conservation only after accounting for explicit time dependence.
- A generator need not be the Hamiltonian. The Hamiltonian generates physical time evolution, while momentum, angular momentum, and other functions generate other canonical transformations.
- Canonical coordinates need not look like ordinary positions and momenta. What matters is that their fundamental brackets have the canonical form.

## Quantum Bridge

Poisson brackets are the classical ancestor of quantum commutators. The structural analogy is:

$$
\lbrace f,g\rbrace \leftrightarrow \frac{1}{i\hbar}[F,G]
$$

This is a structural correspondence, not a rule that turns every classical expression into a unique quantum operator. Operator ordering and other quantization issues can obstruct a literal replacement. The durable lesson is that both brackets encode generators, time evolution, symmetries, and compatibility of observables in their respective theories.
