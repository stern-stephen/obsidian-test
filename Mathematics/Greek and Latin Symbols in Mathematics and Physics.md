# Greek and Latin Symbols in Mathematics and Physics

## Overview

Mathematics and physics reuse a relatively small alphabet for many different quantities. A symbol has no universal meaning by itself: its meaning comes from the surrounding definition, subject, units, and whether it is uppercase, lowercase, bold, or decorated.

This note lists common uses rather than fixed definitions.

## Greek Letter Variants

Some lowercase Greek letters have standard alternate glyphs. The variants usually represent stylistic or field-specific choices, but authors may use both forms as distinct symbols.

| Standard form | Variant | Typical distinction |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $\epsilon$ | $\varepsilon$ | Both mean epsilon; permittivity and small parameters often use either form |
| $\theta$ | $\vartheta$ | Both mean theta; useful when two angle-like quantities must be distinguished |
| $\kappa$ | $\varkappa$ | Both mean kappa; the variant is less common |
| $\pi$ | $\varpi$ | $\pi$ is the circle constant; $\varpi$ often denotes parallax or a modified momentum |
| $\rho$ | $\varrho$ | Both mean rho; either may denote density or a coordinate |
| $\sigma$ | $\varsigma$ | Both mean sigma; $\varsigma$ is uncommon |
| $\phi$ | $\varphi$ | Both mean phi; conventions differ between fields and typefaces |

## Typeface And Decoration Conventions

The same letter can denote different kinds of objects depending on its typography.

| Form | Typical meaning | Example |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Italic scalar | Number, coordinate, or scalar-valued variable | $m$, $t$, $E$ |
| Bold lowercase | Vector | $\mathbf{v}$, $\mathbf{x}$ |
| Bold uppercase | Matrix or tensor | $\mathbf{A}$, $\mathbf{T}$ |
| Arrow | Vector, especially in elementary physics | $\vec{F}$ |
| Hat | Unit vector, estimator, or quantum operator | $\hat{n}$, $\hat{H}$ |
| Overdot | Time derivative | $\dot q$, $\ddot x$ |
| Prime | Derivative, transformed quantity, or related variable | $f'$, $x'$ |
| Bar | Average, complex conjugate, or closure | $\bar{x}$, $\bar{z}$ |
| Tilde | Approximation, transformed quantity, or Fourier-domain quantity | $\tilde f$ |
| Calligraphic | Functional, family, Lagrangian density, or special set | $\mathcal{L}$, $\mathcal{F}$ |
| Blackboard bold | Standard number system or expectation operator | $\mathbb{R}$, $\mathbb{C}$, $\mathbb{E}$ |

## Common Multi-Letter Symbols And Operators

These are written with upright letters because they are names of functions or operators rather than products of variables:

- $\sin$, $\cos$, $\tan$: trigonometric functions
- $\sinh$, $\cosh$, $\tanh$: hyperbolic functions
- $\log$, $\ln$, $\exp$: logarithmic and exponential functions
- $\det$: determinant
- $\dim$: dimension
- $\ker$: kernel
- $\operatorname{tr}$: trace
- $\operatorname{Re}$ and $\operatorname{Im}$: real and imaginary parts
- $\min$, $\max$, $\inf$, $\sup$: extrema and bounds

## Reading A Symbol Reliably

When a symbol is unfamiliar, use this order:

1. Find where the author defines it.
2. Check whether it is uppercase or lowercase and whether it is bold, hatted, barred, or indexed.
3. Inspect its units or dimensions.
4. Identify the subject: the same $T$ may mean temperature, period, kinetic energy, or an operator.
5. Check nearby equations for its mathematical role, such as scalar, vector, matrix, function, or operator.
