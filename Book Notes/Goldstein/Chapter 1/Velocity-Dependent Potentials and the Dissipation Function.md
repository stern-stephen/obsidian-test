## Big Ideas

- Some velocity-dependent forces can be generated by a generalized potential.
- The electromagnetic Lorentz force is the central physical example.
- Linear friction cannot be included in the ordinary potential, but it can be represented by Rayleigh's dissipation function.
- A dissipative Lagrange equation requires both $L$ and the dissipation function.

## Generalized Potential

If generalized forces can be written as:

$$
Q_j=-\frac{\partial U}{\partial q_j}+\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial U}{\partial\dot q_j}
$$

then Lagrange's equations retain their standard form with:

$$
L=T-U
$$

Here $U(q,\dot q,t)$ is a generalized or velocity-dependent potential.

## Electromagnetic Example

The Lorentz force is:

$$
\mathbf{F}=q\left(\mathbf{E}+\mathbf{v}\times\mathbf{B}\right)
$$

Using:

$$
\mathbf{E}=-\nabla\phi-\frac{\partial\mathbf{A}}{\partial t}
$$

$$
\mathbf{B}=\nabla\times\mathbf{A}
$$

a suitable generalized potential is:

$$
U=q\phi-q\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{v}
$$

and the Lagrangian is:

$$
L=\frac{1}{2}mv^2-q\phi+q\mathbf{A}\cdot\mathbf{v}
$$

## Rayleigh Dissipation Function

For drag linear in velocity, define:

$$
\mathcal{F}=\frac{1}{2}\sum_i\left(k_xv_{ix}^2+k_yv_{iy}^2+k_zv_{iz}^2\right)
$$

The friction force is:

$$
\mathbf{F}^{(f)}=-\nabla_{\mathbf{v}}\mathcal{F}
$$

In generalized coordinates:

$$
Q_j^{(f)}=-\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial\dot q_j}
$$

The equations of motion become:

$$
\frac{d}{dt}\frac{\partial L}{\partial\dot q_j}-\frac{\partial L}{\partial q_j}+\frac{\partial\mathcal{F}}{\partial\dot q_j}=0
$$

For the convention used here, $2\mathcal{F}$ is the rate at which mechanical energy is dissipated.

## Common Confusions

- A generalized potential may depend on velocity; an ordinary potential does not.
- The electromagnetic vector potential term changes the canonical momentum.
- Rayleigh's function is not potential energy.
- The dissipation formalism applies directly to forces linear in generalized velocities, not arbitrary friction.
