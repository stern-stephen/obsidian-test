## Big Ideas

- A ket $|v\rangle$ is a vector.
- A bra $\langle v|$ is the corresponding dual vector, a linear functional that takes a ket and returns a scalar.
- Dirac notation makes inner products, projections, basis expansions, and operators compact.
- The adjoint operation turns kets into bras and reverses the order of products.

## Notes

Shankar introduces the dual space through Dirac notation so that vectors, linear functionals, inner products, and operators can be written in one compact language. The important source progression is ket to bra, bra-ket to scalar, and ket-bra to operator.

He then uses an orthonormal basis to introduce completeness and basis expansion. This prepares the continuous-basis formulas of Section 1.10, where sums become integrals and Kronecker deltas become Dirac deltas.

The reusable notation, outer-product interpretation, and expectation-value forms live in [Bra-Ket Notation](../../../Linear%20Algebra/Bra-Ket%20Notation.md). The adjoint rules and operator classes are consolidated in [Adjoints](../../../Linear%20Algebra/Adjoints.md).

## Common Confusions

- A bra is not just a ket written backward. It lives in the dual space and acts on kets.
- The order in $\langle u|v\rangle$ matters in complex vector spaces because $\langle u|v\rangle = \langle v|u\rangle^*$.
- The outer product $|u\rangle\langle v|$ is an operator, not a scalar.

## Exercise Answers

These are answer summaries for Shankar Chapter 1 exercises in this section. I am not reproducing the full problem statements here.

### Exercise 1.3.1

Start with:

$$
|A\rangle=3\hat{i}+4\hat{j}
$$

and:

$$
|B\rangle=2\hat{i}-6\hat{j}
$$

Normalize the first vector:

$$
|e_1\rangle=\frac{1}{5}(3\hat{i}+4\hat{j})
$$

Subtract from $|B\rangle$ its projection along $|e_1\rangle$:

$$
|B_\perp\rangle=|B\rangle-|e_1\rangle\langle e_1|B\rangle
$$

Since:

$$
\langle e_1|B\rangle=\frac{1}{5}(3\cdot2+4\cdot(-6))=-\frac{18}{5}
$$

we get a vector proportional to:

$$
4\hat{i}-3\hat{j}
$$

Thus one orthonormal basis is:

$$
|e_1\rangle=\frac{1}{5}(3\hat{i}+4\hat{j}),\qquad |e_2\rangle=\frac{1}{5}(4\hat{i}-3\hat{j})
$$

Another orthonormal basis can be generated by starting with $|B\rangle$ instead:

$$
|f_1\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}(\hat{i}-3\hat{j}),\qquad |f_2\rangle=\frac{1}{\sqrt{10}}(3\hat{i}+\hat{j})
$$

### Exercise 1.3.2

The point is to apply Gram-Schmidt to Shankar's three starting vectors. The result is the orthonormal set:

$$
|1\rangle=
\begin{bmatrix}
1\\
0\\
0
\end{bmatrix}
$$

$$
|2\rangle=
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
1/\sqrt{5}\\
2/\sqrt{5}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

and:

$$
|3\rangle=
\begin{bmatrix}
0\\
-2/\sqrt{5}\\
1/\sqrt{5}
\end{bmatrix}
$$

The first vector is already normalized. The second is obtained by removing the component along $|1\rangle$ from the second original vector and normalizing. The third is obtained by removing the components along both $|1\rangle$ and $|2\rangle$, then normalizing.

### Exercise 1.3.3

The Schwarz inequality becomes an equality precisely when the two vectors are linearly dependent:

$$
|V\rangle=a|W\rangle
$$

for some scalar $a$. For ordinary arrows, this says equality holds when the two arrows are parallel or antiparallel.

### Exercise 1.3.4

Start with:

$$
\|V+W\|^2=\langle V+W|V+W\rangle
$$

Expand:

$$
\|V+W\|^2=\|V\|^2+\|W\|^2+\langle V|W\rangle+\langle W|V\rangle
$$

The cross terms combine to:

$$
2\operatorname{Re}\langle V|W\rangle
$$

Using:

$$
\operatorname{Re}\langle V|W\rangle \le |\langle V|W\rangle|
$$

and Schwarz:

$$
|\langle V|W\rangle|\le \|V\|\|W\|
$$

we get:

$$
\|V+W\|^2\le \|V\|^2+\|W\|^2+2\|V\|\|W\|
$$

Therefore:

$$
\|V+W\|^2\le (\|V\|+\|W\|)^2
$$

Taking square roots gives the triangle inequality:

$$
\|V+W\|\le \|V\|+\|W\|
$$

Equality requires $|V\rangle=a|W\rangle$ with $a$ real and positive, so that the vectors point in the same direction rather than opposite directions.
