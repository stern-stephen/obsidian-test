## Big Ideas

- Symmetries connect transformations with conserved quantities.
- Spatial translation symmetry is tied to conservation of momentum.
- Rotational symmetry is tied to conservation of angular momentum.
- Time-translation symmetry is tied to conservation of energy.
- This classical story prepares the quantum story where symmetries are represented by operators.

## Notes

A symmetry is a transformation that leaves the physical content of the system unchanged.

For Shankar's purpose, the most important symmetries are continuous transformations: transformations that can be made by an arbitrarily small amount, such as shifting a system slightly in space, rotating it by a tiny angle, or advancing time by a tiny amount. Continuous symmetries are special because they are connected to conserved quantities.

The slogan is:

$$
\text{continuous symmetry} \rightarrow \text{conserved quantity}
$$

This is the classical version of the idea behind Noether's theorem. Shankar presents it in Hamiltonian language because Hamiltonian mechanics already uses phase-space variables and Poisson brackets, which have a close quantum analogue.

In Hamiltonian language, a quantity $G$ is conserved when:

$$
\frac{dG}{dt} = 0
$$

If $G$ has no explicit time dependence, this is equivalent to:

$$
\{G,H\} = 0
$$

This makes conservation laws look algebraic: a conserved quantity has a vanishing Poisson bracket with the Hamiltonian.

## What Counts As A Symmetry

A transformation is not a symmetry merely because it is a convenient relabeling. It is a symmetry when the transformed situation obeys the same physics as the original situation.

For example, if a free particle is moved from one place to another in empty space, nothing physical changes. The laws do not care where the origin is. That is spatial translation symmetry.

But if the particle sits in a nonuniform external potential $V(x)$, shifting the particle changes its physical situation. Then spatial translation symmetry is broken, and momentum need not be conserved.

## Generators

In Hamiltonian mechanics, a quantity can generate an infinitesimal transformation through Poisson brackets.

If $G(q,p)$ is the generator, then a small change with parameter $\epsilon$ changes any phase-space function $f(q,p)$ by:

$$
\delta f=\epsilon \{f,G\}
$$

This equation is easy to read backward:

- $G$ is the quantity that generates the transformation.
- The Poisson bracket tells how each observable changes under that transformation.
- If the Hamiltonian does not change under the transformation, then the transformation is a symmetry.

For spatial translations, the generator is momentum. In one dimension:

$$
\delta x=\epsilon \{x,p\}=\epsilon
$$

and:

$$
\delta p=\epsilon \{p,p\}=0
$$

So momentum generates shifts in position. This is the classical ancestor of the quantum statement that the momentum operator generates translations.

## Why Symmetry Gives Conservation

The Hamiltonian determines time evolution. If a transformation generated by $G$ leaves the Hamiltonian unchanged, then:

$$
\delta H=\epsilon \{H,G\}=0
$$

Using antisymmetry of the Poisson bracket:

$$
\{H,G\}=-\{G,H\}
$$

so:

$$
\{G,H\}=0
$$

If $G$ has no explicit time dependence, Hamiltonian time evolution gives:

$$
\frac{dG}{dt}=\{G,H\}
$$

Therefore:

$$
\frac{dG}{dt}=0
$$

This is the main mechanism of section 2.8: if $G$ generates a symmetry of the Hamiltonian, then $G$ is conserved.

## Core Correspondences

- Spatial translation symmetry gives momentum conservation.
- Rotational symmetry gives angular momentum conservation.
- Time-translation symmetry gives energy conservation.

## Examples

### Spatial Translations

If the Hamiltonian has no dependence on absolute position, then shifting all positions by the same amount changes nothing physical. The conserved quantity is linear momentum.

For a particle with:

$$
H=\frac{p^2}{2m}
$$

there is no $x$ dependence, so:

$$
\frac{dp}{dt}=-\frac{\partial H}{\partial x}=0
$$

Momentum is conserved.

### Rotations

If the Hamiltonian is unchanged by rotations, then no direction in space is preferred. The conserved quantity is angular momentum.

For a central potential $V(r)$, the force always points along the radial direction, so the torque vanishes and angular momentum is conserved. In Hamiltonian language, rotational invariance means the Hamiltonian has zero Poisson bracket with the angular momentum generator.

### Time Translations

If the Hamiltonian has no explicit time dependence, then the same experiment run today or tomorrow follows the same rules. The conserved quantity is energy.

This is why time-translation symmetry is tied to conservation of energy.

## How To Read The Logic

The logic is not:

$$
\text{quantity is conserved} \rightarrow \text{there must be a visual symmetry}
$$

The useful direction is:

$$
\text{Hamiltonian is invariant under a continuous transformation} \rightarrow \text{generator is conserved}
$$

Shankar wants you to see that conservation laws are not separate miracles. They come from invariances of the dynamics.

## Quantum Bridge

In quantum mechanics, the role of Poisson brackets is taken over by commutators. The classical condition:

$$
\{G,H\} = 0
$$

becomes analogous to:

$$
[G,H] = 0
$$

up to the usual factors involving $i\hbar$.

The same idea survives, but the mathematical objects change:

- Classical observables are functions on phase space.
- Quantum observables are operators on Hilbert space.
- Classical transformations are generated by Poisson brackets.
- Quantum transformations are generated by commutators and unitary operators.

For example, classical momentum generates translations through Poisson brackets. Quantum momentum generates translations through a unitary operator built from the momentum operator.

## Common Confusions

- A symmetry is not just visual prettiness; it is an invariance of the equations or action.
- Conservation laws follow from continuous symmetries, not from every possible relabeling.
- A generator is both an observable and a machine for producing a transformation.
- If a transformation changes the Hamiltonian, it is not a symmetry of that system, even if the transformation is mathematically allowed.
- The quantum commutator analogy is structural, but Poisson brackets and commutators live in different mathematical settings.
