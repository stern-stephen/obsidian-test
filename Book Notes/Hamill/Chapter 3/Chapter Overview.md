## Big Ideas

- D'Alembert's principle converts Newton's second law into a virtual-work statement suited to generalized coordinates.
- The Nielsen form of Lagrange's equations uses kinetic energy and generalized forces before assuming forces come from a potential.
- Hamilton's principle states that the physical path in configuration space makes the action stationary.
- The Chapter 2 Euler-Lagrange equation becomes Lagrange's equation when $\Phi$ is replaced by $L$ and $x$ by $t$.
- Multiple generalized coordinates require one Lagrange equation for each independent variation.
- Lagrange multipliers let dependent coordinates remain in the problem and reveal generalized forces of constraint.
- Some non-holonomic constraints can be handled by multiplier methods when written as differential virtual-displacement relations.
- The principle of virtual work removes reaction forces by restricting allowed virtual displacements.
- Lagrange's equations retain their form under point transformations, which justifies choosing convenient generalized coordinates.

## Logical Progression

1. Start from Newton's second law written as a virtual-work identity.
2. Transform Cartesian virtual displacements into generalized-coordinate variations.
3. Use the kinetic-energy identities from Chapter 1 to obtain the Nielsen form.
4. Specialize to conservative forces to recover the usual Lagrange equations.
5. Reinterpret mechanics as a stationary-action problem in configuration space.
6. Apply Chapter 2's variational calculus to derive Lagrange's equations again.
7. Retain constrained coordinates with multipliers and interpret multiplier terms as generalized constraint forces.
8. Extend the multiplier idea to certain non-holonomic differential constraints.
9. Use invariance under point transformations to explain why convenient coordinates can be chosen freely.
