## Overview

Section 2.6 discusses the EPR argument and Bell inequalities.

The main point is that quantum mechanics makes predictions that cannot be reproduced by any theory satisfying certain classical assumptions about pre-existing local properties.

## EPR Idea

Einstein, Podolsky, and Rosen argued that quantum mechanics seemed incomplete because entangled systems allow strong correlations between distant measurements.

The tension comes from trying to keep both:

- Locality: distant systems should not instantly influence each other.
- Realism: measurement outcomes reveal pre-existing properties.

Quantum mechanics does not fit comfortably into this classical picture.

## Bell Inequality

Bell's theorem turns the philosophical tension into an experimentally testable inequality.

If measurement outcomes are determined by pre-existing local properties, then certain correlations must satisfy a bound.

In the CHSH form, the classical assumptions imply an inequality of the form:

$$
\langle QS\rangle + \langle RS\rangle + \langle RT\rangle - \langle QT\rangle \le 2
$$

Quantum mechanics predicts that entangled states can violate this bound.

## Meaning Of The Violation

A Bell inequality violation shows that the correlations produced by quantum mechanics cannot be explained by local hidden variables of the classical kind.

This does not mean quantum mechanics allows faster-than-light communication.

It means that entanglement produces correlations that are stronger than classical local realism allows.

## Why It Matters

Bell inequalities are central because they show that quantum mechanics is not merely a strange mathematical reformulation of classical uncertainty.

They reveal that entanglement has experimentally observable consequences.

This section also connects the conceptual foundations of quantum mechanics to quantum information, where entanglement becomes a resource for protocols and algorithms.
