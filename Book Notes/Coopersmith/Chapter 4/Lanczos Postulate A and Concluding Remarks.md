## Big Ideas

- Coopersmith contrasts the usual applied-force statement of virtual work with Lanczos's reaction-force statement.
- Lanczos's formulation emphasizes the hidden constraint forces rather than the given applied forces.
- The principle is a postulate of analytical mechanics, not a theorem derived purely from Newtonian vector mechanics.
- Coopersmith's concluding emphasis is that work is the primary scalar quantity, while force is secondary.

## Usual Statement

The usual statement of the principle focuses on applied forces:

$$
\sum_i\mathbf{F}_i^{appl}\cdot\delta\mathbf{r}_i=0
$$

This is useful for calculation because the applied forces are typically known.

## Lanczos's Reframing

Lanczos instead highlights the reaction-force condition:

$$
\sum_i\mathbf{F}_i^{reaction}\cdot\delta\mathbf{r}_i=0
$$

Coopersmith uses this to make the role of compatibility explicit: reaction forces do no virtual work only for virtual displacements that obey the kinematic constraints.

Lanczos's Postulate A says, in short, that reaction forces do zero virtual work for virtual displacements compatible with the constraints.

## Concluding Interpretation

Coopersmith argues that virtual work succeeds because it provides a coordinate-independent equilibrium test in an abstract configuration space. The test can ignore internal mechanisms when those mechanisms only enforce ideal constraints.

The chapter ends by motivating the broader variational program: Newtonian force methods are effective in simple rectangular-coordinate problems, but virtual work points toward a more general formulation in which work and energy are more fundamental than force.
